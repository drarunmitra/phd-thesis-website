5 Data Utilization Practices

This section presents a cross-case thematic analysis of data utilization practices among Medical Officers and associated staff at PHCs in ITDA Rampachodavaram. Based on Braun and Clarke’s framework, we developed six subthemes that reflect the lived realities of how maternal and child health (MCH) data is entered, accessed, coordinated, monitored, and acted upon at the PHC level.

5.1 Data Entry and Collection

Across all three PHCs, data entry processes are largely delegated to frontline staff—namely ANMs, MPHEOs, and data entry operators. Medical Officers primarily receive post-entry summaries rather than directly entering data into systems. Despite the existence of structured portals like RCH and ANMOL, much of the initial data collection still happens manually, particularly in tribal areas where connectivity and mobility are limited.

“Digitalization is a very big problem in the tribal areas. Every data is being collected manually and then they will have to digitalize.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

“It’s already consolidated… entered by ANM, ASHAs, MPHEOs… They will consolidate and give us the information.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

Though some officers expressed interest in being more hands-on, logistical constraints and legacy practices kept them at a remove from data entry, highlighting a fragmented assemblage of human actors and digital systems.

5.2 Data Access

Medical Officers across the PHCs faced structural barriers to direct access, particularly to patient-level or line-listed data. Until recently, many lacked their own login credentials for portals like RCH. Access was mediated through other personnel (e.g., Peter at the district level), or through unofficial sources like WhatsApp groups. Even after being granted access, officers encountered usability barriers such as OTP authentication tied to specific mobile numbers, and the absence of easy filtering features.

“Now a medical officer has been given a RCH ID… I still haven’t opened it. I just received the mail ID and password now in the morning.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

“Earlier it was sub-district… now it is each PHC, a separate login ID… separate mobile number for OTP.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

“We don’t have access to everything that Peter keeps.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

Data access, therefore, was more symbolic than functional—data was “available,” but not readily actionable by those in clinical leadership roles.

5.3 Communication and Coordination

Communication within the PHC and across departments relied heavily on unofficial digital channels such as WhatsApp groups, where Excel sheets were circulated. These informal systems were preferred for their speed and familiarity, despite lacking essential patient identifiers. Interdepartmental coordination (e.g., with ICDS, education, or police) was uneven, with Medical Officers often improvising based on personal relationships rather than systematic protocols.

“Monthly report we are not using RCH as of now… in the excel sheet we will be putting it in the WhatsApp group.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

“Some ANM are putting ANMO globally… but on and on it is not so… and they want to change that… but they don’t have any control.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram

This reliance on ad-hoc coordination structures further complicates any standardized, scalable approach to data utilization.

5.4 Data Tracking and Monitoring

Data use was largely episodic and compliance-driven. Officers used data primarily to prepare for Performance Oversight (PO) meetings, rather than for real-time monitoring or planning. They lacked tools to track pending cases across categories like immunization, ANC follow-up, or PNC visits. Even when reports were generated, they often lacked line listings or were non-interactive.

“We already have everything. We will put everything into place in that day [for PO meetings].” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

“If they tell you which are pending, then you can say that this is done… and so he can recalculate.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram

This reflects a form of reactive data practice, where information is used to answer to higher authorities, not to guide day-to-day clinical or public health decisions.

5.5 Connectivity and Accessibility

Connectivity emerged as a foundational constraint, shaping when and how data could be uploaded, accessed, or updated. Both PHC Boduluru and Vadapalli reported significant challenges in mobile and internet access, especially during field visits. These infrastructural limitations fed into broader delays in data entry, validation, and feedback cycles.

“There is no signal in the field… we just note it down and update when we return.” — Paraphrased insight from multiple PHCs

“We need to take someone else’s help to segregate it and then allot it to someone else.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

These disruptions extend beyond inconvenience—they reflect structural deterritorializations in the data assemblage that prevent continuity between service delivery and digital reporting.

5.6 Staff Engagement and Support

Finally, data utilization was closely linked to staff capacity and comfort with technology. Some Medical Officers and ANMs reported limited orientation to digital systems. One-day orientations were perceived as insufficient, and ongoing support was largely absent. Despite these constraints, there was a visible desire among staff to improve their data-related skills—particularly for task planning, patient follow-up, and decision-making.

“Orientation was for one day… there is no proper information about these portals and applications.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram

“Actually, we had consolidated reports almost every alternate day.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru

Even when engagement is high, the current systems don’t support staff in building long-term, meaningful data practices that align with their professional judgement or local contextual needs.

5.7 Cross-PHC Comparison

While all three PHCs showed common patterns of constrained and delegated data use, some contextual nuances stood out. In PHC Gangavaram, where the Medical Officer is from the local community, there was slightly more initiative in interpreting data despite low portal comfort. PHC Boduluru reported highly manual systems due to extreme connectivity issues. PHC Vadapalli showed the clearest example of recent shifts—Medical Officers had only just received personal login credentials, indicating a transition stage toward more direct engagement. Across all sites, however, the structural logic of data flow remained the same: fragmented, mediated, and reactive.

6 Challenges in Data Utilization

While the infrastructure for digital health systems has expanded across the tribal PHCs of ITDA Rampachodavaram, significant challenges continue to constrain the ability of healthcare providers to use data effectively for clinical and administrative decision-making. Through our action research engagement, we identified seven interrelated domains of challenge that illustrate the limitations of current systems and practices. These domains span system design, infrastructure, human behavior, and inter-organizational coordination—underscoring that data use is not a standalone task, but one embedded within a broader assemblage of social, technical, and institutional dependencies.

6.1 1. Portal Usability and System Complexity

A persistent challenge across all PHCs is the cumbersome nature of health data portals such as RCH and ANMOL. Medical Officers described a fragmented access structure, where each PHC has its own login ID linked to a unique mobile number, requiring OTP authentication for each session. Navigating the portals often involves clicking through multiple pages without intuitive design, and the inability to edit or retrieve specific datasets (such as line lists or filtered categories) limits user control.

“No, there is no edit option after we have entered the diagnosis. We will have to send a sample by hook or crook.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli
“Earlier it was sub-district… now it is each PHC, a separate login ID… separate mobile number for OTP.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

The system complexity creates a high barrier to entry for Medical Officers, especially when paired with limited digital training. The portals are not designed for real-time clinical engagement, but rather appear structured around administrative aggregation—alienating frontline users who require speed, specificity, and minimal friction in urgent contexts.

6.2 2. Delayed Access and Lack of Real-Time Decision Support

PHC teams operate in a data ecosystem characterized by lags and intermediation. Medical Officers often do not have immediate access to relevant data—such as immunization or ANC updates—and instead rely on field staff like ANMs or MPHEOs to supply information. However, these staff are themselves working in disconnected areas, without mobile signal, and must later return to the center to upload data. This delay undermines real-time decision-making, especially in clinical or follow-up scenarios.

“We have no real-time view of pending cases or who is due… We just prepare for PO meetings with whatever we are given.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram
“The field staff are outside, but the system wants GPS tagging from the field. They cannot do it there due to network issues, so they come back to the center… but then they cannot do fieldwork.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru

This cycle produces a constant tension: either staff are in the field doing service delivery or in the center doing data entry. The lack of synchrony and real-time visibility inhibits prompt responses to urgent needs, weakening the overall service responsiveness of the PHC.

6.3 3. Training Gaps and Knowledge Deficits

There is a pervasive sense among staff that the introduction of digital systems was not accompanied by adequate capacity building. Training sessions, where conducted, were often described as superficial, one-day events that focused more on procedures than conceptual clarity or problem-solving. Many officers and field staff learned portal use through informal trial-and-error, often relying on peers rather than institutional support.

“Orientation was for one day… there is no proper information about these portals and applications.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram
“We just learned by doing… no one showed us how to use filters or get reports.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru “No one explained the logic behind the indicators… we just enter and forward.” — DEO, Vadapalli

This knowledge vacuum leads to inconsistent practices, incomplete entries, and low confidence in using data proactively for local-level planning or clinical improvement.

6.4 4. Connectivity and Infrastructure Limitations

The technological backbone necessary for digital data work remains underdeveloped in many tribal PHCs. Poor or non-existent mobile network access in field areas means that real-time data entry (especially in ANMOL, which requires GPS tagging) is impossible at the point of care. Staff must return to the PHC or a connected zone to upload records, introducing delays and breaking the continuity of care. Additionally, device limitations—shared computers, old smartphones—compound the infrastructural fragility.

“There is no signal in the field… we just note it down and update when we return.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru
“Sometimes the server is down, or the login fails… We delay everything.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

These constraints are not just logistical—they produce a persistent structural lag between service delivery and data reflection, weakening trust in the system.

6.5 5. Operational Overload and Workflow Conflicts

Medical Officers and support staff are routinely expected to manage multiple demanding tasks: clinical consultations, field supervision, health reporting, interdepartmental coordination, and digital data entry. These competing responsibilities generate friction. For example, GPS-based requirements in ANMOL require field-level presence to record services, but the absence of connectivity means field staff often have to physically return to the PHC to complete data uploads—forcing a trade-off between service provision and documentation.

“We are expected to see OP, check deliveries, supervise field work, and do data… We can’t do everything at once.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram
“There’s only one computer and many users. So we just wait or skip the data part.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru “Transportation is also an issue. Sometimes we don’t have anyone to go with to the field. That affects whether data gets collected at all.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

This misalignment between clinical logic and data system requirements adds cognitive and temporal load, often pushing data entry to the margins.

6.6 6. Cultural, Behavioral, and Interdepartmental Misalignments

Behavioral resistance and coordination breakdowns further complicate data-driven care. Medical Officers reported that some ANMs and ASHAs were reluctant or slow to adopt new portal procedures, especially those requiring field-based data capture or digital literacy. In parallel, volunteers were often unresponsive, and intersectoral collaboration with ICDS, education, or village secretariats was inconsistent.

“Some ANMs are still not comfortable with ANMOL. They say it doesn’t work in the field, so they don’t use it much.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram
“Volunteers are supposed to help, but they are not active… we cannot force them.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram “We need coordination from all the teams… schools, ICDS, even police sometimes.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

These behavioral and institutional mismatches disrupt the feedback loops that make data meaningful and actionable.

6.7 7. Data Quality and Integrity Concerns

Finally, concerns about the accuracy, consistency, and trustworthiness of the data itself were widespread. Errors during data entry, failure of synchronization between systems (e.g., ANMOL and RCH), and an absence of validation mechanisms contribute to doubts about the reliability of what is entered and what appears in reports. Medical Officers often lacked a way to verify or correct discrepancies, nor did they receive confirmation that their data was used meaningfully by higher levels.

“The ANMs complain that the ANMOL data is not synchronizing to the RCH portal.” — Medical Officer, Gangavaram
“We don’t know if the numbers are correct… sometimes things disappear from the dashboard.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

These issues weaken the perceived value of data work and reduce motivation to maintain rigor in its collection and entry.

6.8 Cross-PHC Comparision

While structural barriers to effective data utilization were present across all PHCs, the manifestations of these challenges varied by local context. In PHC Vadapalli, Medical Officers were in a transitional phase—only recently granted access to the RCH portal—yet were expected to respond to real-time performance reviews. In PHC Boduluru, severe connectivity constraints amplified dependence on manual workarounds, while operational burden was high due to limited staff. PHC Gangavaram demonstrated slightly more coordination autonomy, but challenges around training, synchronization issues, and multitasking were deeply felt. Across all sites, digital systems remained non-integrated with clinical workflows, resulting in fragmented, delayed, and burdened data engagement.

7 Data Utilization Needs

In the tribal healthcare context of ITDA Rampachodavaram, the effective use of maternal and child health (MCH) data is closely linked not just to the availability of systems, but to how those systems interface with people, roles, and infrastructural realities. Through our interviews, it became evident that staff across all three PHCs hold a vision of more responsive, contextualized, and user-centered data systems. Their needs, expressed both explicitly and implicitly, suggest a desire to move from passive data reporting to active, informed decision-making at the local level. These needs span technical, infrastructural, organizational, and relational domains.

7.1 Data Accessibility and Usability

One of the most frequently expressed needs was access to detailed, patient-specific, and task-relevant data. Rather than aggregated numbers shared over WhatsApp or in PDF summaries, Medical Officers described the importance of line-listed data—organized by village, ASHA, or service type—for targeted follow-up and planning. Without this granularity, it is difficult to assign responsibilities, monitor progress, or act proactively.

Additionally, there was a clear demand for simplified user interfaces. Staff reported frustration with the current design of portals like RCH, where essential information is buried within layers of navigation and the reporting formats often do not align with the immediate informational needs at the PHC level.

“We don’t know who are pending and from which Sachivalayam… we need to assign properly.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli
“Even if they have the data, they don’t know where to enter the data.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru

This subtheme reflects a broader concern that the structure of available data often serves supervisory oversight rather than operational responsiveness.

7.2 Real-Time Data Interaction

Staff across all PHCs expressed a strong desire for real-time tools that could alert them to pending services, delays in data entry, or exceptions in service delivery. In the current system, information flows are fragmented and delayed. ANMs and ASHAs often update portals only after returning from the field, leading to a time lag in data visibility.

Real-time interaction was not simply about technological novelty; it was about enabling responsive healthcare. A dashboard or mobile alert system, updated daily, was envisioned as a tool for tracking progress, redistributing tasks, and preparing for supervisory meetings.

“If a dashboard could give alerts via WhatsApp… that would be better than logging in every time.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru

This reflects a broader orientation toward actionable visibility—data that is timely, relevant, and integrated into the work rhythm.

7.3 Training and Capacity Building

Despite ongoing digitalization, training remains minimal and episodic. Staff described receiving one-off orientation sessions—often focused more on data entry compliance than on portal navigation, indicator interpretation, or problem-solving strategies.

Grassroots health workers, such as ASHAs and ANMs, face particular challenges. Many of them lack confidence with mobile apps and struggle with synchronization or input errors, especially when working in low-connectivity zones. Respondents called for not just more training, but contextualized, ongoing support that addresses their specific challenges.

“Orientation was for one day… we need training on how to actually manage databases.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru

This subtheme suggests that without investing in the human infrastructure of data systems, digital platforms will remain underutilized or misused.

7.4 Infrastructure and Resource Support

Fundamental infrastructural deficits continue to undermine data utilization. Mobile network coverage is inconsistent, especially in PHCs like Boduluru and parts of Vadapalli, where staff must travel back to the PHC or other locations just to synchronize data. These limitations also impact GPS-enabled tasks in ANMOL and delay real-time uploads.

There is also a need for updated devices and support mechanisms to manage high-stakes data demands during official reviews or inspections. Medical Officers described the stress of preparing for performance evaluations without timely access to clean, accurate, or consolidated data.

“Proper telephone signals would make it more helpful for ASHAs.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli
“If we have the data beforehand, we could improve before even going to the meeting.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru

In this context, basic infrastructure becomes a prerequisite for digital accountability.

7.5 Planning and Feedback Mechanism

Respondents repeatedly pointed to the need for better planning tools—daily checklists, service tracking calendars, and automated summaries that could support micro-level monitoring. These tools would allow staff to pace their work, prevent end-of-month backlogs, and respond to emerging service gaps.

In addition to task-oriented tools, participants emphasized the importance of feedback mechanisms that go beyond fault-finding. There was a desire for appreciation, affirmation, and constructive feedback that recognized the constraints under which they work.

“We will have a deadline so that we will be on track.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli
“Such appreciation is missing… even if it is a small thing.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru

This reveals an emotional and motivational dimension to data work—where recognition reinforces engagement and retention.

7.6 Empowerment and Delegation

Several Medical Officers called for more autonomy in interpreting and responding to data. Currently, they often receive pre-filtered or incomplete data, limiting their ability to make decisions based on local priorities. There were also concerns about blurred role boundaries: some felt they were overloaded with data entry responsibilities, while others described how field staff were unclear on what data to capture and how to interpret it.

The need here is twofold: better role clarity and greater decision-making latitude at the PHC level. This subtheme connects directly to broader questions of institutional trust and the decentralization of authority in public health governance.

“We should be able to monitor the work done by the ANMs… and plan accordingly.” — Medical Officer, Boduluru
“We do not know what’s happening at the Anganwadi center… we cannot monitor follow-ups.” — Medical Officer, Vadapalli

This speaks to a structural desire for redistributing data power—from district and state levels, back to the frontlines of care.

7.7 Cross-PHC Comparision

Although all three PHCs articulated shared needs for infrastructure, training, and usability improvements, the degree of emphasis and specificity varied. PHC Vadapalli demonstrated a strong demand for real-time tools and delegation systems, reflecting a growing readiness for digital transition. PHC Boduluru focused heavily on infrastructure, planning mechanisms, and practical feedback loops—underscoring how service delivery in disconnected areas relies on local innovation. PHC Gangavaram, while less vocal on workflow systems, placed more emphasis on training and support for grassroots workers. These distinctions suggest that while structural reform is broadly needed, localized tailoring of data solutions will be essential for effectiveness.
